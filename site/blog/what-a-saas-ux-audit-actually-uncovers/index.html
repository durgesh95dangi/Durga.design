What a SaaS UX Audit Actually Uncovers: The Curse of Knowledge, the 4 Heuristic Lenses and How Findings Become a Prioritized Dev Roadmap

Your team tested everything. It all works. So why are people still dropping off? This is what an audit finds.

Durga PrasadSenior Product Designer · 8+ years in fintech & B2B SaaS

Quick answer

A SaaS UX audit reviews a product through usability heuristics such as information architecture, interaction cost, feedback and error prevention. It finds the friction founders and teams stop noticing because they know the product too well, and turns each finding into a list of fixes ranked by business impact and engineering effort.

Key takeaways

  • Teams stop seeing their own UX problems. That's the curse of knowledge, not carelessness.
  • A good audit looks at findability, effort, feedback and error prevention.
  • Every finding should be ranked by business impact vs engineering effort.
  • Start with quick wins that take an afternoon and unblock many users.

When a founder books a UX audit with me, they usually say something like this:

"Our developers tested everything. It all works. But conversion is stuck and we can't see where people drop off."

Are those founders careless? Not at all. They're just too close to the product.

Why can't founders see their own product's UX problems?

It's called the curse of knowledge.

After a year inside your codebase and Figma files, you know every shortcut. You know you have to click Save before Next. You know the dropdown takes a second to load. You know where export lives, because you put it there.

A new user knows none of that. They arrive cold and click where logic says they should.

When the product does something unexpected, they don't blame themselves. They close the tab.

What does a SaaS UX audit check?

I don't give vague feedback like "make this blue." I go through the main funnel with four lenses, built on Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics:

  1. Information architecture: can people find things? Or are key settings buried four menus deep?
  2. Interaction cost: how much effort does the core job take? If it takes 9 clicks and could take 2, people get tired.
  3. Feedback and visibility: does the product respond? Every action should get an immediate, clear reaction.
  4. Error prevention: are big mistakes hard to make? Deleting a whole project should never be one click.

How are UX audit findings prioritized?

The worst thing a consultant can do is hand over a 50-page PDF of complaints with no order. That just demoralizes the team.

In practice, every finding goes on a simple 2x2 grid: business impact vs engineering effort.

Quick wins go first: copy changes, button styles and small layout fixes that take an afternoon but unblock a lot of users. Bigger structural fixes get planned into sprints.

What do you get at the end of a UX audit?

It depends on who runs it, but you should expect three things: each problem shown in context, suggested fixes (ideally as wireframes rather than only words), and a prioritized list your developers can start on the next day.

My own audit includes a screen-by-screen video teardown, Figma fixes for the top friction points and a prioritized dev roadmap, delivered in 36 hours.

The takeaway

You can't unsee your own product. That's normal.

Get fresh eyes on it, then ship the quick wins first.

FAQ

What is a UX audit?

A UX audit is an expert review of a product against usability principles to find where users get stuck. It ends with a prioritized list of fixes.

How long does a SaaS UX audit take?

It depends on the scope. A focused audit of one core funnel can take a day or two; a full product audit takes longer. My fixed-scope audit is delivered in 36 hours.

What's the difference between a UX audit and usability testing?

An audit is an expert review against known principles. Usability testing watches real users try tasks. Audits are faster and cheaper; testing shows behavior nobody predicted. They work best together.
